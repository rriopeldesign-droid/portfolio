Lifecycle & Email Campaigns

Designing the full email lifecycle from welcome sequences to re-engagement campaigns. Building scalable templates in Braze and establishing a visual language that drove measurable retention.

Scope

Over two years, I designed every customer-facing email RVezy sent, from transactional confirmations and marketing campaigns to seasonal promotions, host communications, re-engagement sequences and referral flows. It was never a one-off project. It was an ongoing system that grew with the business.

Once everything was designed in Figma, we built a modular email template system in Braze. Instead of designing each email from scratch, we put every campaign together from the same set of blocks, like hero modules, listing cards, calls to action and testimonial strips. A new campaign took hours to assemble instead of days.

The Lifecycle Map

I mapped every touchpoint in the guest and host journeys where email played a role. The guest lifecycle alone had 23 distinct email touchpoints. The host side had another 18.

Three of them mattered more than the rest, so that’s where we put most of our effort. Those were the 48 hours after a guest searched but didn’t book, the day before a trip, and a new host’s first week after their listing went live.

The Template System

Modular blocks

Every email was built from a library of 12 reusable blocks, including hero images, listing grids, single call-to-action sections, testimonials and pricing callouts. Each block was tested across 30+ email clients and had a dark mode version.

Brand consistency at scale

Before the system, every campaign was a one-off design. After it, every email followed the same guidelines, no matter who on the marketing team put it together. Nobody had to start from a blank file anymore, though each email still came back to me for a design review.

Constraints

Designing for the inbox, not the browser

Email is one of the most hostile design surfaces there is. Layouts are built with tables, many clients block images by default, some versions of Outlook render with a word-processor engine, and dark mode can invert your colors without asking. A polished Figma comp means nothing if half the audience sees a broken version of it.

So we designed the system around those constraints from day one. Layouts were single-column so they’d degrade gracefully, text stayed live instead of being baked into images, alt text was written as real copy, and colors were chosen to survive automatic dark-mode inversion. Every block was tested across clients before it went into the library.

Key Moments

Three moments that mattered most

  • After a search without a booking. Within 48 hours, guests received the RVs they had viewed, with live availability and pricing pulled in, plus similar options nearby. The goal was to feel like a helpful reminder, not a chase.
  • The day before a trip. First-time renters are anxious. This email gathered pickup details, a pre-trip checklist and the host’s contact into one place, cutting last-minute support calls and setting up a good first trip.
  • A host’s first week. New listings that don’t get booked early tend to go quiet. A short coaching sequence nudged hosts to add photos, adjust pricing and enable instant book, the same actions the product later surfaced in the Host Dashboard.
Measurement

Measuring what actually mattered

Early on, success was judged by open rate. That metric became unreliable as mail privacy features started inflating opens, so I worked with our data analyst to move the team toward clicks, bookings and GMV attributed to each campaign. Key flows ran with holdout groups, which is how we could credibly attribute the $1.2M in incremental GMV rather than counting revenue that would have happened anyway.

That shift changed the creative work too. Instead of debating subject lines in isolation, we tested whole structures (one call to action versus several, listing-led versus story-led) and let the results shape the next round of templates.

Collaboration

Working with CRM, data and engineering

Design was only one part of each campaign. I worked with our CRM manager on segmentation and trigger logic in Braze, so the right email reached the right person at the right moment. A front-end developer hand-coded some of the core blocks and stepped in whenever an email broke in Braze.

Compliance shaped the work too. Canadian anti-spam rules set clear expectations around consent, sender identification and unsubscribing, so those elements were designed into every template rather than added at the end.

Our data analyst set up the reporting that tied campaigns to bookings, and we reviewed results together after each major send. Those reviews decided which blocks stayed in the library, which were redesigned, and which quietly disappeared.

The Gaps

Consistent, but far from solved

The system did what it was built to do. Every email followed the same guidelines and looked like it came from the same company, and building from blocks made campaigns about 4× faster to produce. The process around it was a different story.

Most emails were still designed in Figma and exported with a plugin. The plugin gave us raw HTML we could take into Braze, but once it got there it often broke or didn’t resize properly on phones, and we’d need engineering to step in again. Every new block or custom component meant another trip through Figma and the plugin. Light and dark mode were never really solved either. An email that looked right in one inbox could be unreadable in the next.

So even with every block documented and starter templates for the most common campaigns, each email still needed a designer to look it over before it went out. The work was standardized, but it still ran through me.

What Came Next

The tool I couldn’t build in 2022

Fixing the process meant replacing the whole chain, not just the templates, and in 2022 the AI tooling to build something like that on my own didn’t exist yet. By 2025 it did, and I built the Email Builder in a few days.

It keeps the idea that worked here, emails made from on-brand blocks, and drops everything around it that kept breaking. There’s no Figma file and no export plugin. Every block has a light and a dark palette, and the HTML comes out ready for Braze. Marketing picks a template, edits it in one place, checks it in light and dark mode, then copies the HTML into Braze.
